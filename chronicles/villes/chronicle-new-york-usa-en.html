At 7:15 in the morning on the F train, between Carroll Street and Smith–9th Streets, the car crosses a black cast-iron viaduct above the Brooklyn rooftops. Below, the raking sun draws long shadows on the brownstones. Across the water, Manhattan rises through the haze. A man in a hard hat sleeps standing up, hand clamped to the pole. A woman in a suit works through a file on her phone, sneakers on her feet. Three teenagers share a pair of AirPods. This car is an entire planet — Caribbean, Latino, Asian, European, all of it standing together in the same steel box on the way to work. Nowhere else in the world does human density produce this impression: that the city is made of a different material, more compressed than anywhere else, where every square kilometre contains as many life trajectories as an entire country.

New York, the impossible city that keeps existing

New York is the largest city in the United States (8.3 million residents across the 5 boroughs, 20 million in the metropolitan area) and the country's economic and cultural capital — despite Washington for politics, Los Angeles for entertainment and San Francisco for tech. It concentrates the world's largest banks, its most active financial markets, its richest cultural institutions, its most influential media. It also hosts 800 different languages — according to the city's Department of Education, no other human agglomeration on Earth counts as many. That figure explains New York better than any other statistic: it is not an American city. It is a country unto itself.

The 5 boroughs are not interchangeable. Manhattan is the financial, media and cultural core — dense, vertical, extraordinarily expensive, and far less representative of actual New York life than is imagined from the outside. Brooklyn has become over twenty years the epicentre of American creative culture — from artists and entrepreneurs to Haitian, Bangladeshi and Ecuadorian immigrant families who have never left their neighbourhoods in three generations. Queens is the world's most nationally diverse county. The Bronx, birthplace of hip-hop, has an energy and history that most new arrivals overlook. Staten Island, accessible only by ferry or bridge, is a semi-suburban breath that many New Yorkers have never visited.

For an expat, the first and most important decision is not "New York or somewhere else." It is "which borough, which neighbourhood." Living in Manhattan and living in Astoria (Queens) are two entirely different experiences of the same-named city.

Brooklyn — Williamsburg / Bushwick
The creative heart. Bars, galleries, artist studios, vegan restaurants, independent music. 1BR ~$2,600–3,400. Close to Manhattan (L train). The most popular choice for young expats.
Brooklyn — Park Slope / Carroll Gardens
Families, brownstones, Prospect Park, farmers markets. Calmer than Williamsburg. 1BR ~$2,800–3,600. The "grown-up" Brooklyn for expats with children.
Manhattan — Upper West Side / Harlem
The Manhattan compromise. Central Park on foot, real neighbourhood fabric. Harlem still relatively affordable for Manhattan: 1BR ~$2,200–2,900. Authentic culture and energy.
Queens — Astoria / Long Island City
Best value in NYC. LIC is 15 min from Midtown, Astoria has its Greek community and one of the most diverse food scenes in NYC. 1BR ~$1,900–2,600.
Manhattan — Lower East Side / Chelsea
For those who want Manhattan without paying Midtown prices. Culture, nightlife, galleries. 1BR ~$3,200–4,500. The prices reflect the density of what happens within walking distance.
The Bronx — Riverdale / Fordham
The most affordable borough with metro access to Manhattan. 1BR ~$1,500–2,200. Less taken up by expats but authentically New York. For those optimising absolute value.

Housing: the unfiltered reality

Let's be direct: New York is one of the most expensive cities in the world for housing. As of March 2026, the median rent for a 1-bedroom apartment in Manhattan runs around $4,250–4,800/month (RentHop, Zumper). Central Brooklyn (Williamsburg, Park Slope): $2,800–3,400. Queens (Astoria, LIC): $1,900–2,600. The Bronx: $1,500–2,200. These figures are for free-market apartments — NYC has a rent-stabilised system covering roughly 44% of the rental stock, with potentially much lower rents, but accessing it takes time and patience.

New York move-in costs are equally significant. The market standard is: first month + last month + one month's security deposit — three months upfront. That means $8,400–14,400 to move into a 1BR in Brooklyn, and up to $14,400–28,800 in Manhattan (last month is not always required, but security deposit and broker fees often are). Broker fees — historically 15% of a year's rent — were capped in 2025 at one month's rent by New York State law, a meaningful improvement for new arrivals.

⚠️ Healthcare: the issue that surprises every non-American

The American healthcare system has no equivalent in Europe, Canada or Japan. There is no universal coverage. An uninsured expat can face a $2,000–5,000 bill for an emergency room visit, and far more for hospitalisation or surgery. Individual health insurance (via the ACA/Marketplace) costs $300–800/month for a healthy adult in New York, depending on coverage level. This is a non-negotiable expense to factor into any New York expat budget, and it genuinely shocks people from countries with universal coverage who are encountering the US system for the first time.

New York cannot be understood from the outside. It is experienced from within — through its streets, its subways, its neighbours. The city you imagine before arriving is almost never the one in which you end up living.

Working from New York

New York is the largest US job market in finance, media, fashion, advertising, law, real estate and significant portions of tech (the rest being in San Francisco). The median annual salary in the city is approximately $75,000/year (BLS 2025) — roughly ~$5,000/month net for a mid-level profile after federal, state and city taxes. New York City taxes income at three stacked levels — federal, New York State, and NYC City tax — making effective rates among the highest in the country. A senior in finance, tech or law: $120,000–250,000/year. An entry-level media or arts role: $45,000–65,000/year — often not enough to live alone in Manhattan.

For digital nomads, New York presents a paradox. The infrastructure is excellent: gigabit fibre available, premium coworkings everywhere (WeWork, Industrious, The Wing, Primary — dozens of options), cafés with wifi on every block. But the visa question is the real barrier. The United States has no digital nomad or remote worker visa. A foreign national can spend up to 90 days as a tourist (ESTA/visa waiver) but cannot legally work from US soil for a foreign employer under that status — at least not while receiving US-sourced compensation. Legal long-stay options: O-1 visa (extraordinary ability), L-1 (intracompany transfer), E-2 (investor visa, available by treaty, accessible to nationals of many countries), or employer sponsorship (H-1B, whose annual lottery sharply limits access). New York is reachable — but requires serious visa planning.

New York's startup scene is America's second after the Bay Area. Dozens of accelerators (Y Combinator has a strong presence, Techstars NY, ERA), thousands of venture funds, and a concentration of talent in fintech, adtech, e-commerce, healthtech and digital media that is unlike anything outside California.

Culture, nights and diversity

New York is the cultural capital of the United States — not an opinion, an institutional and economic fact. It holds the Metropolitan Museum of Art (the Met), the MoMA, the Whitney, the Guggenheim, the Brooklyn Museum, the American Museum of Natural History — a concentration of world-class collections that only Paris can match. Broadway is the world's largest commercial theatre market. Carnegie Hall, the Lincoln Center, and the David Geffen Hall for classical music. The Jazz Standard, Smalls, Blue Note for jazz in 80-seat rooms where the world's finest musicians play four nights a week.

But what genuinely makes New York unique for those who live there is the density of the informal: poetry readings in Brooklyn basements, Thursday evening openings at Chelsea galleries, Bronx block parties in summer, Sunday farmers markets at Union Square where you recognise the same faces after five years, underground parties in Bushwick lofts with no name that never repeat. This city creates daily rituals that nobody planned and nobody forgets.

New York nightlife is not the world's most raucous — it was in the 1970s–80s when Studio 54, the Paradise Garage and the Limelight defined an era. Today it is diverse and inexhaustible: Bushwick techno clubs open until 10am, West Village jazz bars drinking until 4am, Queens bodegas open around the clock. There is no legal last-call mandate in New York bars — technically the limit is 4am, often ignored.

Anecdotes & History

The unwritten rules of the New York subway are an anthropology in themselves. Never talk too loudly in a packed car. Let people off before you get on. Give up your seat for the elderly, pregnant or those with children — without being asked. Never hold eye contact too long. And yet — at very specific, inexplicable moments — deep conversations between strangers who will never meet again. The MTA subway (founded 1904) moves 3.5 million people daily through a network of 472 stations, the largest in the world by stop count. It is loud, often late, sometimes dirty, and absolutely essential. New York without its subway would not be New York.

James Baldwin (1924–1987) — born in Harlem to a poor family, the ninth of nine children — is the writer who best captured New York's fundamental tension: a city that promises everything and distributes unequally. His novels (Go Tell It on the Mountain, Giovanni's Room, Another Country) and essays (The Fire Next Time, Notes of a Native Son) are written from New York, about New York, about America as seen from Harlem — and they have not aged. Baldwin left New York for Paris in 1948, unable to endure American racism any further. He returned, left again, returned again. New York defines him precisely because he left it and came back. "You think your pain and your heartbreak are unprecedented in the history of the world," he wrote, "but then you read." Baldwin read in New York, at the 42nd Street Public Library, which is still there.

Who is New York for?

💼 Employed professional
✅

The American city par excellence for a career in finance, media, law, fashion, adtech. Salaries among the world's highest in these sectors. H-1B or L-1 visa via employer essentially required.

🚀 Entrepreneur / startup
✅

Exceptional VC ecosystem, dense networks, access to decision-makers. E-2 (investor) or O-1 (talent) visa for non-sponsored founders. High operating costs but real capital available.

💻 Digital nomad
⚠️

Perfect infrastructure but visa is the real barrier. 90 days on ESTA possible, not easily renewable. For a long, legal stay, a precise legal status is mandatory — not optional.

👨‍👩‍👧 Family
⚠️

Viable with a high budget. Public schools vary enormously by neighbourhood. Private schools: $30,000–60,000/year per child. Very limited space for the price paid. Queens or Brooklyn suit better than Manhattan.

WiggMap Verdict

New York: the price of intensity

New York is the only city in the world where you can simultaneously hear 5 languages crossing one block, eat the world's best pizza in a basement for $3 a slice, and attend a world-premiere theatre production two hours later. That cultural and human density has no price — but it does have a rent, taxes, health insurance and a visa. And all four are high.

The verdict: New York is the American destination for the ambitious, the creative, the entrepreneurs and the professionals whose sector is concentrated here. It is one of the most intense living experiences on earth. And it is a city that does not forgive unprepared budgets.

✓ Strengths

  • Unmatched cultural density worldwide
  • Premium sector job market
  • Human diversity: 800 languages, everything exists here
  • World gastronomy — every cuisine on earth
  • Energy, opportunity, constant serendipity

  • Massive international expat community

  • Visa: no nomad visa, work visa complex and competitive

  • Exhausting pace over the long term
  • Safety varies significantly by neighbourhood and time

Frequently asked questions

What visa do you need to live and work legally in New York?
The US has no digital nomad visa. Main options: H-1B (skilled worker — requires employer sponsorship + annual lottery, highly competitive); L-1 (intracompany transfer — requires a multinational employer transferring you); O-1 (extraordinary ability — for artists, researchers, entrepreneurs with demonstrable achievements); E-2 (investor visa — requires a substantial investment in a US business, available by treaty to nationals of many countries, including UK, France, Germany, Australia, and others but not all); EB-1/EB-2 (priority green cards). For short stays (tourism/conference), ESTA allows 90 days without a visa. Working remotely for a foreign company while on ESTA is a legal grey zone that should be addressed with a qualified immigration attorney before arriving.
How do you find an apartment in New York as a foreigner?
The main difficulty for a foreigner: landlords typically require annual income equal to 40 times the monthly rent ($134,400/year for a 1BR at $2,800/month) and a US credit history. Without a US credit score, solutions include: working with a broker who has landlord relationships familiar with foreigners; offering 2–3 months of additional security deposit; using platforms specialised for new arrivals like Furnished Finder, Sonder, or June Homes for the first months. Most accessible neighbourhoods for expats without credit history: Astoria (Queens), Sunset Park (Brooklyn), Washington Heights (north Manhattan). Never pay deposits for apartments you haven't visited in person — rental scams targeting foreigners are unfortunately common.
How does healthcare work in New York for an expat?
Three scenarios: (1) Employed by a US company → health insurance typically offered by employer (partially or fully), standard at large companies. (2) Freelance/entrepreneur/self-employed → must purchase individual insurance. Via the NY State of Health Marketplace (ACA), plans range from ~$250/month (bronze, high deductible) to ~$800/month (gold, broad coverage). Essential to understand: premium (monthly payment), deductible (annual out-of-pocket before coverage kicks in), copay (fixed portion per visit) and out-of-pocket maximum (annual spending cap). (3) Tourist/short stay → international travel insurance mandatory, with a minimum $100,000 medical coverage strongly recommended. European national health systems do not cover medical costs in the US.
What is a realistic monthly budget for a comfortable life in New York?
For a single person in a 1BR in Brooklyn (Williamsburg/Park Slope): Rent: $2,800–3,200. Health insurance: $350–600. Utilities (electricity + internet): $130–180. Transport (monthly MetroCard): $132. Food (groceries + reasonable dining out): $600–900. Miscellaneous (outings, gym, subscriptions): $300–500. Total estimated: $4,312–5,512/month, or $52,000–66,000/year. For Manhattan (Lower East Side, minimum sensible): add $800–1,500/month in rent. Note: NYC taxes represent approximately 30–35% of gross income for an $80,000/year earner (federal + NY State + NYC City tax combined).
